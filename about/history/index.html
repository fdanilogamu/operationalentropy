History of OEI

Operational Entropy Index did not begin as a complete methodology. It developed as specific operational problems exposed questions the existing framework could not yet answer. This is the short version of how those questions shaped OEI, and eventually the Institute responsible for its continued development.

  1. March 2026

    Operational entropy gets a name

    Years of systems and operations work converged into a single idea: recurring operational problems that looked unrelated could be understood as forms of entropy. That recognition became operational entropy, and then the Operational Entropy Index, initially as a way to diagnose operational drag.

    This was catalyzed by:

    Writing “I May Have Typecast Myself” brought ideas from different books into contact with roughly 15 years of professional experience. The recognition was simple: “I’m someone who notices entropy.”

  2. April-June 2026

    Diagnosis becomes methodology

    OEI expanded beyond identifying operational drag. The four-day Diagnosis developed five dimensions, scoring, deeper audit and intervention pathways, while work on diagnostic reporting produced concepts such as the Mitigation Trap.

    This was catalyzed by:

    Trying to make OEI produce an actual diagnostic artifact exposed something important: a system can appear healthy because people are continually compensating for its weaknesses. Apparent functionality and structural health were not the same thing.

  3. June-August 2026

    The framework gets sharper boundaries

    The dimensions became more precise, particularly Workflow Velocity, while OEI also developed a clearer account of how internal operational conditions become externally visible.

    This was catalyzed by:

    Concrete work experiences kept testing the boundaries of the framework. Work stalled when completing it required leaving the “workbench” for missing context, while client experience revealed itself not as another internal dimension but as a downstream signal of the five that already existed.

    A branch becomes another invention

    The realization that people can reintroduce entropy into improved systems produced a separate line of work around systemic fit. That work became Entropy Compatible Hiring (ECH), a separate invention that branched from a problem encountered while developing OEI.

  4. August 2026

    Evidence becomes methodology

    OEI’s diagnostic process became explicitly evidence-led:

    Context → Evidence → Validation → Synthesis → Diagnosis

    Claims remained attributable, contradictions and uncertainty remained visible, and missing evidence could itself become diagnostically relevant.

    This was catalyzed by:

    Building the Diagnosis as an actual workspace exposed a problem: founder claims, staff reports, and direct operational evidence could not safely be treated as equivalent facts. The methodology needed to preserve the difference between what someone said, what could be validated, and what the evidence ultimately supported.

  5. Late August 2026

    OEI encounters its own dependency problem

    OEI began acquiring practitioner documentation, competency models, assessment infrastructure, authority boundaries, canon governance, and systems for propagating established methodology.

    This was catalyzed by:

    OEI itself depended on its founder. A methodology capable of identifying Founder Dependency in organizations still required Fletcher to execute and interpret it. The question became how OEI could remain OEI when practiced by somebody else.

  6. September 2026

    OEI becomes an institution

    The emerging pieces became the OEI Institute: not simply a consulting organization, but a body responsible for governing the methodology, developing practitioners, assessing competence, maintaining standards, and enabling bounded independent practice.

    This was catalyzed by:

    Work on making OEI replicable had already produced much of the necessary institutional machinery. When an attempted collaboration around scaling OEI as replicable consulting ended, the unresolved question became clearer: perhaps the thing that needed to scale was not Fletcher’s consulting practice at all. The event helped crystallize the distinction between scaling a consulting practice and governing a methodology. The resulting insight was: “Train specific consultant types.”

The methodology is still allowed to change

OEI Institute exists partly to make that change governable. The canon can develop as practice produces new evidence, while preserving the distinction between established methodology, proposed changes, and individual practitioner judgment.
